Most people hope that technology will free us from work. We welcome machines that lift heavy objects, software that handles repetitive paperwork, and tools that make difficult tasks easier. Yet when the work in question is intellectual, the hope often changes into fear. We want less drudgery, but we are uneasy about machines that can think and use tools.

That unease is understandable. Our jobs are not just sources of income; they give us a sense of being useful. If AI can do the work that once made us valuable, what happens to us?

But these are reasons to take the transition seriously, not reasons to pretend we want intellectual labor to remain necessary. If a system could help anyone learn, reason, design, diagnose, write, or solve problems at a level that once required years of specialized training, that would be an extraordinary expansion of human capability. AI serves to make expertise less scarce—and to let people spend less of their lives doing work simply because no one else can do it.

That is why, if we genuinely want liberation from intellectual labor, we should be rooting for AGI. AGI is not liberation by itself. It is a possibility: that the ability to think, create, and solve problems could become abundant enough to change what society asks of people. We should root for realizing that possibility, while insisting that its benefits serve humanity broadly and that the transition is worthy of the people living through it.
